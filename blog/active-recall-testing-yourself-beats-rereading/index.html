Active Recall: Why Testing Yourself Beats Rereading

Rereading feels productive, yet testing yourself is one of the most reliable ways to learn. Here is why, and how to start.

By the Rosjos team November 17, 2026 6 min read

Rereading your notes feels productive. The pages look familiar, and familiarity creates a sense of confidence. The problem is that familiarity isn't the same as knowing something. Active recall, which means pulling information out of your memory instead of reviewing it again, is one of the most reliable study methods researchers have tested.

Why rereading can mislead you

When you reread a chapter, the material looks familiar, so it's easy to assume you understand it. But recognizing an idea on the page is different from explaining it from scratch during an exam. Rereading tells you what seems familiar. Testing shows you what you can actually retrieve.

What the research shows

In a well-known 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared students who repeatedly reread a passage with students who read it once and then practiced recalling it. The students who practiced recall remembered noticeably more a week later. Many studies since then have found similar patterns, which is why retrieval practice is now widely recommended.

How to practice recall

  • Close your notes and write everything you remember about a topic, then check what you missed.
  • Explain a concept out loud, as if teaching someone who has never heard of it.
  • Answer practice questions before looking at the worked solutions.
  • Use flashcards in both directions, so you practice the term and the definition.

Turn your notes into questions

Before studying a section, turn its headings into questions. Answer each question from memory, then compare your answers with the text. Return to the questions you missed after a day or two. Spacing your practice this way strengthens memory more than cramming the same material into one sitting.

About learning styles

You may have heard that people learn best through a preferred style, such as visual or auditory learning. Research has not supported the idea that matching instruction to a learning style improves results. What does help is the kind of practice you do, and active recall works for nearly every subject.

Start small

Choose one chapter this week and try active recall on it. Notice how quickly you find the gaps. Then apply the same method to your next test. Once it feels natural, you'll have a routine that works whether you're preparing for a quiz or a final exam.
