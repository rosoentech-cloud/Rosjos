A Four-Week Study Plan for Standardized Tests

Four weeks is enough time for steady, measurable progress. This plan moves from diagnosis to full practice in a clear sequence.

By the Rosjos team November 17, 2026 6 min read

Standardized tests reward preparation that is steady and well targeted. A four-week plan gives you enough time to find your weak spots, improve them, and build the stamina you need for test day. The plan below assumes you can study several times a week. Adjust the daily time to fit your schedule.

Week one: find your starting point

Begin with a full or near-full practice test under timed conditions. Score each section, then note which question types you missed most often. Group your misses into categories, such as content you don't know, questions you misread, and questions you ran out of time on. That list becomes your study plan for the next three weeks.

Week two: rebuild the foundations

Spend most of this week on your two or three weakest areas. For each one, review the core concepts, study one or two worked examples, and then solve a set of practice questions without help. Keep sessions focused, and review every question you got wrong before moving on.

Week three: practice under pressure

Shift toward timed practice. Work through sets of questions with a clock running, and track how long you spend on each one. If you consistently run out of time, practice the question types you're slow on until your pace improves. Review every missed question carefully, because the explanation often reveals a pattern you can fix.

Week four: full practice and rest

Take one or two full practice tests early in the week, under conditions as close to the real test as you can manage. Use the last two days for light review of your error log and key strategies. Avoid learning new material at the end, and get a full night's sleep before test day.

A daily routine that works

  • Study in focused sessions of about sixty to ninety minutes, with short breaks.
  • Mix topics within a session so you practice switching between skills, as the real test requires.
  • Keep a simple log of scores and time spent, so you can see your progress.
  • Sort out test-day logistics early: registration, identification, travel route, and timing.

Get help where you're stuck

If the same kind of question keeps tripping you up, that's a signal to get targeted help. A tutor can identify the gap behind the mistake, explain the concept from a different angle, and set practice that builds confidence before the real exam.
